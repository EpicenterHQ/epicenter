Three questions that make any chart readable

Ask them before you read a single number, and most misleading charts fall apart on the first one — usually within five seconds.

01

What is one mark?

Every dot, bar or slice stands for one thing. Name that thing out loud before anything else.

A bar might be one country, one person, one day, or one survey response — and a chart that mixes them is not a chart, it is a picture. This question alone catches most of what goes wrong, because a chart whose mark you cannot name is usually hiding that it has more than one.

If you cannot finish the sentence “each dot is one…”, stop reading the chart. Nothing below this question is worth answering yet.

02

What does distance mean?

Find out what is being turned into length, position or area — and whether it starts at zero.

Length compares honestly only from a zero baseline. Position on a line does not need one. Area is the one people read worst: doubling a circle's radius quadruples what your eye sees, which is why bubbles flatter big numbers and shrink small ones.

Same two numbers, both times. Only the baseline moved. If a chart uses length and hides its zero, it is making an argument, not showing data.

03

What is missing?

Charts are made of what someone chose to include. Ask what the frame cuts off.

Three things go missing most often: the years before the ones shown, the denominator that would turn a count into a rate, and the cases that were dropped for being incomplete. None of them leaves a mark on the page, which is exactly why you have to ask.

The solid line is a recovery, not a rise — but only the dashed part tells you that, and the dashed part was not in the chart.

Ask them in order. Most charts fail on the first one.

And when one fails, you are done — there is no reason to work out what the numbers say until you know what a mark is.
