Eight figures · one accent each

What the diagrams look like

No boxes, no legends, no second colour — everything is carried by line, weight and space.

Each one below is drawn the way the house style requires, with a note on the job it does. The constraint that shapes all of them: a diagram gets one accent, so it can mark exactly one thing.

Ranked bars

When the order is the point and the numbers are secondary.

The rule at work: the accent marks the row you should act on. Every other bar is one neutral grey, because they are not categories — they are the same quantity at different sizes.

Never give each bar its own colour. If the bars are already labelled, colour adds nothing and spends the one mark you had.

Two lines over time

When the shape of a change matters more than any single value.

The rule at work: the baseline is thinner and grey, the line you are arguing about is thicker and accented. Weight does the work a legend would otherwise do.

A sequence on one rule

When steps happen in order and one of them is where things go wrong.

The rule at work: the gaps follow how long each step takes — compressed, so the short ones stay visible, but never reordered. The bottleneck is obvious before you read a number. A row of equal boxes and arrows would hide it.

Watch for this. Most flow diagrams space their steps evenly, which quietly asserts they cost the same.

Nesting, without nested boxes

When one thing contains another and you need the depth to be obvious.

The rule at work: indent plus a vertical rule reads as containment just as well as a border, and the levels stay legible past three deep, where nested boxes stop working.

Part to whole

When the reader needs to see a share, not compare separate quantities.

The rule at work: one bar, not a pie, and one segment accented because it is the one you can shrink. The lightest grey is the remainder — a third tone, not a third colour.

Two axes, no quadrant boxes

When the lesson is where things sit relative to each other.

The rule at work: no quadrant fills and no labelled boxes. The axes are enough, and the accent says which dot the page is arguing for.

Four tinted quadrants is the most common version of this chart and it spends four colours to say something the axes already said.

Before and after

When the change is the subject and both states must stay comparable.

The rule at work: identical axes and identical scale, so the shapes are honestly comparable. Dashed grey for the old state, solid accent for the new — line style carrying what a second colour usually does.

A hierarchy

When the structure branches and the reader needs to find one leaf.

The rule at work: elbows and indent instead of boxes and connectors. Branches you are not arguing about drop to grey, so the path to the accented leaf is the only thing in full contrast.

One habit covers most of it: before adding a colour, ask whether weight, dash, size or position could carry it instead. They almost always can, and the accent stays available for the one thing that matters.
