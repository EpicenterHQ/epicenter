Four things that make learning stick

All four are uncomfortable while you do them. That is not a coincidence — it is the mechanism.

Almost everyone studies by reading something again. It is the most common method, one of the least effective, and it feels like the best one, which is the entire problem.

Why it feels good. The second read is easy, and your brain reports that ease as understanding. Psychologists call it fluency. It is a measurement of how familiar the page looks, not of what you could do without it.

The four things below all break that comfort on purpose. Each one makes the session feel worse and the month feel better, and they are ordered by how much of that trade they buy.

01

Close the book and try to remember

Pulling a fact out of memory strengthens it far more than putting it in again. Reading a chapter twice and reading it once then writing down what you can recall take the same time, and the second produces dramatically more a week later.

This is the single largest lever on the page. Everything below is a way of arranging it.

Instead of rereading your notes, shut them and write the page from memory. Then open them and see what you missed.

The struggle is the point. If recall is effortless, no learning is happening — you already had it. The difficulty is the work being done, not evidence that it isn't working.

02

Leave gaps between sessions

The same four hours spread across four weeks beat four hours in one evening, and it is not close. Cramming works for Friday and is mostly gone by the following Friday.

The reason is uncomfortable in the same way: you have to forget a little before the review does anything. Reviewing something you still remember perfectly is nearly free, and buys nearly nothing.

Instead of one long session, do four short ones far enough apart that the start of each feels slightly hard.

03

Shuffle the problems

Doing twenty of the same kind of problem in a row teaches you to run one procedure. Mixing kinds forces you to work out which procedure applies, which is the part you will actually need later, when nothing is labelled.

Blocked practice feels smoother and scores better during the session. That is exactly why people keep choosing it.

Instead of finishing all of chapter 4, take a few problems from chapters 1 through 4 and shuffle them.

Not only for studying. The same result shows up in sport and music practice, where blocked repetition also produces a better session and a worse performance.

04

Say why, in your own words

After you learn something, explain how it connects to what you already knew — and where it contradicts it. New facts hooked onto old structure have far more ways to be found again.

This is the real reason explaining a subject to a beginner works so well. The beginner is incidental; the work is in the rephrasing.

Instead of highlighting the sentence, write one of your own saying why it is true.

If you change one thing

Stop rereading. Close the book and write down what you remember, then check. That one swap is most of the benefit on this page, and you can start it this afternoon.

The other three make it compound. But they are refinements of the same move, and none of them helps if you are still reviewing instead of retrieving.
