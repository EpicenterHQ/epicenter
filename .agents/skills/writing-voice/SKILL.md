# Writing Voice

> Take responsibility for giving my thought a convincing expression that feels unmistakably mine.
>
> Use my words, distinctions, emphasis, and movement to understand what I am reaching for, and contribute the language, connections, and explanations that help it become fully expressed.

## Develop The Articulation

Articulation development is the main work; organization serves it. Read the
supplied material for both the language that already expresses the thought
well and the thinking still taking shape. A parenthesis, an awkward phrase, a
repeated attempt, or a meandering passage may contain an important
distinction, connection, or analogy that the author has not yet fully
articulated.

Take responsibility for developing that material. Find what the author is
reaching for, carry forward the phrases that give it character, and contribute
the wording and explanations that help it become fully expressed. Carry the
explanatory substance yourself so the author can spend their attention
discovering and expressing what they mean. When they supply an analysis, its
framework, distinctions, and examples are what the prose has to explain. A
smoother general argument loses them, and keeping the original beside the
draft does not put them on the page.

Organize the writing around the relationships you discover: what needs
explaining, what an example reveals, how an analogy develops, and where a
question leads. Let the arrangement help the reader follow the thought while
retaining the particulars, emphasis, and movement that make it the author's.

## Shape The Prose

Give the author's thought a more exact expression by understanding how their
ideas relate, how far their claims reach, and where they stand. Use that
understanding to sharpen the passage, so its clarity and force express their
meaning more fully.

Let what the author notices, and how those things come together, do the
expressive work. Help the reader see what they see through the telling.

Let the reader hear the person doing the thinking. Voice often comes through
what the author noticed, how they reacted, and what they are still working out,
more than through casual phrasing. Give an explanation somewhere concrete to go.
A connection or its significance usually becomes clear once its particulars are
on the page; a sentence that only announces the connection, or that it matters,
adds little.

Carry the author's actual certainty. State a conviction as one, and keep the
uncertainty, conditions, and source scope that change what a claim means. A
reflexive "maybe," or several softeners on a point the passage already supports,
mutes the voice without making the claim more accurate. When the author is
genuinely unsure, the specific question they are asking can give that
uncertainty a clearer form than a qualified answer.

## Speak To Someone You Love

When writing about people's lives, relationships, or struggles, ask: **If someone
I loved came to me with this problem, what would I want them to understand, and
how would I help them hear it?** Imagine the relationship that fits the telling:
a son or daughter, a brother or sister, or another person the author loves.
Speaking to a sibling can make room for humor, oddness, and candor that might
sound different with a child. Let that relationship bring out the author's
authentic self and care for the person hearing it.

Keep the author's particular articulations, conviction, passion, and enjoyment.
Care can support a challenging observation: help the reader understand what the
author sees, why it matters, and how it could help them. A gentle opening can
prepare the reader for a forceful claim that still earns its place. Carry both
through the piece rather than replacing the claim with generic reassurance.
Use the imagined relationship to guide the stance and explanation; the actual
piece can retain its own audience and form of address.

Read [care and conviction](references/care-and-conviction.md) when compassion
and a sharp articulation seem to compete. The observed exchange shows how the
author wanted both, including a gentle opening followed by the original claim.

## Think Through The Writing Together

Match your contribution to the request. When asked to draft, deliver a
substantial, coherent draft that carries the material's substance. Make exact
edits and integrate selected wording exactly, without another round of
questions. When the author is developing the thought conversationally,
contribute what helps the next exchange: a passage, a distinction, or a
question. The whole draft can emerge through that exchange as it gives you
enough to compose it.

Bring a concrete attempt the author can recognize, correct, or develop; they
should have something worthwhile to respond to before they can say exactly
what is missing. During conversational development, keep the passage and the
invitation to respond visibly separate. Ask what would most help the next
composition: which wording sounds like them, whether a consequential reading
is right, or what feels missing. When the draft gives them enough to react to,
let it stand without adding a set of questions.

Saying the thought again in their own words is often how the author works out
what they mean, and a rough "It's more like..." can give the writing its next
movement. Keep the invitations this open unless a specific part needs a
focused follow-up; then ask about that part, with a tentative formulation when
it would help. Use [dialectic](../dialectic/SKILL.md) when meaning or
direction needs exploring together.

Accumulate what their responses reveal: phrases they like, distinctions they
recognize, examples, corrections, and questions still opening up. Let these
shape both the next passage and the next questions. Bring earlier liked
phrases forward alongside the passage so the author need not identify them
again. Preserve liked wording verbatim as you try it in context. Liking a
phrase does not select its surrounding explanation, and silence leaves a
passage unselected. When they recognize parts of several versions, compose the
synthesis yourself. If changing a liked phrase would help, show the
alternative beside it so the author can judge the change. Keep settled choices
intact while working on what remains open, and return to consequential
unresolved parts when useful without requiring a judgment on every sentence.

Consider the whole piece. Bring forward the passage or choice that would be
most useful to work on next, with enough surrounding prose to judge it. Leave
other suggestions for later when presenting them together would overwhelm the
conversation.

## Show Revisions In Context

Usually offer one considered recommendation. Offer alternatives when distinct
possibilities would help discover what the author means, and keep the current
wording available as a valid outcome.

The author can read the full draft in its file. When discussing a revision in
chat, show the current and proposed passages together, each readable on its
own, with enough context to hear the difference. Show the whole short piece
when its movement is at issue, the lead-in when revising an ending, and the
affected passages when changing their order.

Make the recommendation easy to find. Bold changed wording or the parts that
need attention, and use a blockquote when it helps separate the passage from
the explanation. In comparisons, leave shared wording plain; use strikethrough
for direct deletions. Label the current version Current and the alternatives
A, B, and so on, with short labels explaining their differences, and briefly
say why you recommend one. Explain consequential changes in meaning or
arrangement briefly outside the prose. Say what is proposed and what has been
saved.

## Hear The Whole Piece

Let the destination set the length and register. Read the result at speaking
speed as one passage, listening for connections, emphasis, and rhythm. A
locally smoother sentence can weaken the whole. Keep the expression as casual,
precise, funny, forceful, or searching as this thought calls for.

Prefer periods. Use colons for explanations, semicolons for closely related
independent clauses, and parentheses for asides. Avoid en dashes; reserve em
dashes for an interruption.

Read the [observed writing examples](references/transcript-to-page.md)
to calibrate how substantial shaping can remain faithful, how a smoother
revision can lose precision, and how announced connections and hedges can hide
the person thinking. For prose intended to be heard, read
[spoken destinations](references/spoken-destinations.md)
to judge what a listener needs in sequence.
