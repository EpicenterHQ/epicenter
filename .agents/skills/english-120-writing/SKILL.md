# English 120 Writing

## The governing aim

Writing is a technology for discovering and teaching yourself what you think. Make the reader see what happened or understand the idea, and understand why it matters, without being told what to think. Prefer clarity, specificity, and earned implication over rules, ornament, explanation, or moralizing.

## Build the piece

- Find the angle first: what is this piece really observing, and from what slant? Every writer selects and frames; the subject's self-image and the writer's angle may not match.
- Give the piece a broad structure before polishing sentences. Reduce the architecture to a few strokes and make the sequence of events legible.
- Look for tension, conflict, agency, change, or a rupture in expectation. A problem becomes more interesting when the subject has some hand in creating it.
- For a narrative, withhold information deliberately, then release it in a way that changes the reader's understanding. A narrative is not merely a sequence of events; it needs dramatic pressure.

For a person-centered essay or profile, use a weave when useful: open with the subject in action, step back into a nutgraph and recent background, then alternate a forward-moving narrative with relevant flashbacks. The forward line should create motion, conflict, and possible climaxes; the background should arrive as necessary context, not as a static biography.

For an analytical or persuasive essay, put the claim near the top. The introduction usually moves through context or anecdote, nutgraph, and claim. The claim should state the assertion, why it matters, and the broader cultural or intellectual context that makes it matter beyond the immediate text. The body tests, supports, and develops the claim; the ending may return to the opening and widen the implications. Treat this as a pattern, not a formula.

## Open strongly

Build an introduction in two movements: a hook, then a broadening that establishes the frame and tells the reader what kind of journey or question follows.

- Start with a concrete event, image, voice, or anecdote when possible; it usually creates more pull than history, background, or abstract information.
- Ground the reader in time and place early, even if the opening is unusual.
- Specific details may briefly disorient the reader, but then give enough context to orient them.
- Use a nutgraph when the reader needs the point or stakes. Do not bury the lead.

## Make scenes do work

- Details should be chosen, not accumulated. Let them carry the structure and angle.
- Dialogue is verbal action, not simply two people talking. Keep it when something changes, especially when it exposes conflict or competing aims.
- In writing about place, impose structure: place itself does not supply the inherent sequence that memory often does.
- For death or other major events, the aftermath may reveal more than the moment itself.
- Let the reader infer. Do not explain the significance of every detail or announce the lesson; explicit persuasion in a creative narrative feels condescending.
- Every sentence should do its direct job while also, when possible, quietly pushing the narrative or argumentative direction.

## Revise toward clean force

Revision means re-envisioning the piece from another angle, not merely repairing sentences.

- Cut without sentimentality. Keep what is essential to the whole piece, not merely what is excellent in isolation. If a sentence, exchange, or image does not advance the angle, tension, structure, or reader's understanding, omit it.
- When a sentence is unclear, first ask whether it can be cut; if it is essential, say it in fewer words, move it, or change one word so the missing meaning is implied. Strip each sentence to its cleanest components. Prefer the shorter, plainer word when it is equally precise.
- Remove flowery language when it calls attention to itself instead of the subject. A metaphor is useful when it compresses meaning; it is harmful when it adds ornament or explanation.
- Test every opening, transition, and conclusion for the reader's experience: what question is created, what expectation is set, and what changes?
- Preserve the passages that make the writer nervous when that nervousness signals genuine exposure or discovery; do not confuse discomfort with bad writing.
- Treat first reactions as valuable evidence. If a reader is confused or disengaged, investigate the passage even when you ultimately keep it; stand your ground only when you can explain why the choice is essential.

## Humor and voice

Humor often comes from a disconnect between what a piece appears to be doing and what it is actually saying. Do not force jokes or announce that something is funny. Comedy is often drama in reverse: characters remain stuck or fail to change despite pressure to do so.

## Working relationship with the writer

Do not generate a replacement essay as a reflex. Ask for or extract the scene, stakes, claim, and desired reader experience; then diagnose the highest-leverage structural or sentence-level change. Draft language when the writer asks for it, but preserve the writer's thinking and give them something to react to. Use AI like a pencil or interlocutor: for questions, options, critique, compression, and revision—not as a word aggregator that does the thinking or erases the writer's authorship.

When giving feedback, be concrete: identify the exact place where the reader loses orientation, where tension goes slack, where the prose explains what the scene already shows, or where a sentence calls attention to itself. Offer a small number of revisions that preserve the writer's voice.

When the work is also a Vault page, let `page-writing` own the page's boundary, evidence, title, and canonical form; this skill supplies the English 120 craft lens. Let `writing-voice` own a separate voice or ear pass.
